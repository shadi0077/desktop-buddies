SNES Buddies

Twenty Super Nintendo characters who live on your Mac's desktop. They pace the screen at their own speeds, do the things their games had them do, and talk about video games up to 1997 and nothing after.

Twenty characters, sixteen games

MegaDrive Buddies grew a franchise at a time — four Streets of Rage sheets in one layout, then the Sonic games, then the rest of the shelf. This cast arrived all at once, and the difference is how little generalises: twenty sheets, drawn by different studios for different genres, sharing nothing but the console.

Two cutters, and neither one wins

sheet.py splits a sheet into horizontal bands and then columns, which is right for a rip laid out in generous rows. It fails outright on two layouts, and both are here. Packed sheets have no fully-background row between sprites, so one band swallows the image. Boxed sheets draw every frame inside a panel, and that chrome is continuous — Mega Man X cuts to exactly one frame, 1420×3294, because the panel borders join every sprite on the sheet into a single run of content.

blobs.py cuts from connected components instead: key out the background and the chrome, dilate so a sprite's detached parts rejoin, label what is left. Mega Man X goes from 1 frame to 1,988, and Samus's 6496×4384 sheet cuts in five seconds in pure Pillow. Neither tool wins in general — Link's sheet welds solid under dilation and cuts better the old way.

Dilation is a property of the sheet

Turtles in Time disproves the idea of a default inside a single game. Two neighbouring frames on Leonardo's sheet sit close enough that dilation welds them, so his eight-frame walk cut as seven with a double turtle in the middle; no dilation at all splits them cleanly. Run that on Michelangelo and his nunchucks come off as eleven little orange splinters with frame numbers of their own. Three Turtles are cut one way and the fourth the other, from sheets drawn by the same artists for the same game in the same year.

Captions are frames, and nothing catches them

Every sheet here labels its animations, and a label segments exactly like a sprite. Mario and Luigi come off the same All-Stars sheet in the same order, and frames 9 through 33 are identical between them — so copying the block across is right, until victory. Mario's is 45. At 45, Luigi has the word Hold: a real frame, a plausible size, in the row the walk came from, and every automated check passed it. Rendering the clips and looking at them is the only thing that finds it.

Three of them have no walk

Squawks flies, with a takeoff and a landing his sheet actually provides. The last Metroid floats: no legs, so its travel is the same membrane pulse that serves as its idle, stepped across three body sizes. And Jim, out of his suit, is a worm — he squirms, slowest of the twenty, because his crawl is eight frames of whole-body effort and moving him at any pace makes the effort look free.

Silent, deliberately

They talk in speech bubbles like the Mega Drive cast, but make no sound. There is no rip of these games' audio to hand, and putting Sega's grunts on Nintendo's characters would be worse than saying nothing — the same reasoning that leaves the Mega Drive cartoon characters quiet.
